### Section 1: Pedagogical Theory, Microscopic Foundations & Core Definitions

***

#### 1.1 Atomic & Molecular Nature of Matter (Historical & Physical Context)
**Priority Tag:** ⭐⭐⭐ (Conceptual Foundations & Dimensional Analysis)

Matter is discrete, not continuous. The macroscopic thermodynamic variables—Pressure ($P$), Volume ($V$), and Temperature ($T$)—are statistical averages resulting from the dynamical motions and interactions of an enormous number of microscopic constituents (atoms or molecules).

* **The Atomic Hypothesis:** Formulated quantitatively by John Dalton (1808) via the Laws of Chemical Combination:
  1. *Law of Definite Proportions:* Any given chemical compound always contains its component elements in fixed ratio (by mass).
  2. *Law of Multiple Proportions:* When two elements combine to form more than one compound, the masses of one element that combine with a fixed mass of the other are in the ratio of small whole numbers.
* **Avogadro's Hypothesis (1811):** Equal volumes of all gases under identical conditions of temperature and pressure contain equal numbers of molecules.
* **Size of Molecular Constituents:** Typical atomic diameters are on the order of:
  

$$
d \approx 1\text{ to }2 \text{ Å} = 10^{-10}\text{ to }2 \times 10^{-10}\text{ m}
$$

* **Mean Intermolecular Separation ($r_0$):**
  * In Solids and Liquids: Molecules are closely packed. The mean intermolecular separation is approximately equal to the molecular diameter:
    

$$
r_{\text{condensed}} \approx 10^{-10}\text{ m} = 0.1\text{ nm}
$$

  * In Gases at Standard Temperature and Pressure ($\text{STP}: T = 273.15\text{ K}, P = 1.013 \times 10^5\text{ Pa}$):
    Molecules move freely through empty space. The average separation between gas molecules is:
    

$$
r_{\text{gas}} \approx 10 \times d \approx 10^{-9}\text{ to }3 \times 10^{-9}\text{ m} = 1\text{ to }3\text{ nm}
$$

    Consequently, the volume occupied by the gas molecules themselves is roughly $\left(\frac{1}{10}\right)^3 = 10^{-3} = 0.1\%$ of the total macroscopic container volume. The remaining $99.9\%$ is empty space.

***

#### 1.2 The Concept of an Ideal (Perfect) Gas
**Priority Tag:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (Core CBSE/Board & Competitive Physics Focus)

An **Ideal Gas** is a theoretical construct serving as a baseline limiting model for real gas behavior. It is strictly governed by two fundamental microscopic postulates:
1. **Negligible Molecular Volume:** The physical volume occupied by the gas molecules themselves is strictly negligible compared to the total volume $V$ of the enclosing container.
2. **Negligible Intermolecular Forces:** There are no intermolecular attractive or repulsive forces between the gas molecules (i.e., potential energy $U = 0$). Intermolecular forces exist exclusively during elastic collisions of negligible duration.

> **Condition for Real Gases to Behave Ideally:**
> Real gases consist of molecules with finite size and weak Van der Waals attractions. A real gas approaches ideal gas behavior under:
> 

$$
\mathbf{\text{Low Pressure } (P \to 0) \quad \text{and} \quad \text{High Temperature } (T \gg T_c)}
$$

> * **Why Low Pressure?** When pressure is low, the volume is very large; hence the mean intermolecular separation $r \gg d$, making finite molecular volume negligible.
> * **Why High Temperature?** High temperature means high average kinetic energy ($\frac{1}{2}m v_{\text{rms}}^2 \propto T$). The kinetic energy vastly dominates over any mutual intermolecular potential energy ($E_k \gg |U_{\text{intermolecular}}|$), rendering intermolecular attractions ineffective.

### Section 2: The Empirical Gas Laws

***

#### 2.1 Boyle's Law (Robert Boyle, 1662)
**Priority Tag:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

* **Statement:** For a fixed mass (or constant number of moles $n$) of an ideal gas held at a constant temperature $T$ (isothermal process), the pressure $P$ of the gas is inversely proportional to its volume $V$.
* **Mathematical Form:**
  

$$
P \propto \frac{1}{V} \quad \implies \quad PV = \text{Constant} \quad (\text{when } m, T = \text{constant})
$$

  

$$
P_1 V_1 = P_2 V_2
$$

* **Graphical Signatures:**
  * **$P$ vs $V$ (Isotherms):** Rectangular hyperbolas in the first quadrant. As temperature increases ($T_3 > T_2 > T_1$), the isotherm shifts outward from the origin.
  * **$P$ vs $1/V$:** Straight lines passing through the origin. The slope of the line equals $nRT$; hence steeper lines represent higher temperatures.
  * **$PV$ vs $P$:** Horizontal straight lines parallel to the pressure axis.

***

#### 2.2 Charles's Law (Jacques Charles, 1787 & Gay-Lussac, 1802)
**Priority Tag:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

* **Statement:** For a fixed mass (constant $n$) of an ideal gas kept at constant pressure $P$ (isobaric process), the volume $V$ of the gas is directly proportional to its absolute temperature $T$ (measured in Kelvin).
* **Mathematical Form:**
  

$$
V \propto T \quad \implies \quad \frac{V}{T} = \text{Constant} \quad (\text{when } m, P = \text{constant})
$$

  

$$
\frac{V_1}{T_1} = \frac{V_2}{T_2}
$$

* **Historical Temperature Coefficient of Volume ($\alpha_v$):**
  At constant pressure, for every $1^\circ\text{C}$ rise in temperature, the volume of a gas expands by $\frac{1}{273.15}$ of its original volume at $0^\circ\text{C}$:
  

$$
V_t = V_0 \left(1 + \frac{t}{273.15}\right)
$$

  Where $t$ is the temperature in degrees Celsius ($^\circ\text{C}$), and $V_0$ is the volume at $0^\circ\text{C}$.
* **Absolute Zero Concept:** Setting $V_t = 0$:
  

$$
1 + \frac{t}{273.15} = 0 \implies t = -273.15^\circ\text{C} \equiv 0\text{ K}
$$

  Absolute zero ($0\text{ K}$) is the theoretical temperature at which the volume of an ideal gas would extrapolate to zero. Real gases liquefy/solidify long before reaching this limit.
* **Graphical Signature:**
  * **$V$ vs $T\text{ (Kelvin)}$:** Straight lines passing through the origin $(0,0)$.
  * **$V$ vs $t\text{ }(^\circ\text{C})$:** Straight lines with positive vertical intercept $V_0$ at $0^\circ\text{C}$ and negative horizontal intercept at $-273.15^\circ\text{C}$.
  * Higher isobar curves ($P_1 < P_2 < P_3$) have smaller slopes since $\text{Slope} = \frac{nR}{P}$.

***

#### 2.3 Gay-Lussac’s Law / Amontons's Law of Pressure-Temperature
**Priority Tag:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐

* **Statement:** For a fixed mass of an ideal gas held at constant volume $V$ (isochoric process), the pressure $P$ is directly proportional to its absolute temperature $T$.
* **Mathematical Form:**
  

$$
P \propto T \quad \implies \quad \frac{P}{T} = \text{Constant} \quad (\text{when } m, V = \text{constant})
$$

  

$$
\frac{P_1}{T_1} = \frac{P_2}{T_2}
$$

  

$$
P_t = P_0 \left(1 + \frac{t}{273.15}\right)
$$

* **Graphical Signature:** Straight lines (isochores) on a $P-T$ plane intersecting the origin $(0\text{ K})$. As container volume decreases, the slope $\left(\frac{nR}{V}\right)$ increases.

***

#### 2.4 Avogadro’s Law
**Priority Tag:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐

* **Statement:** Under identical conditions of temperature and pressure, equal volumes of all ideal gases contain an identical number of molecules ($N$).
* **Mathematical Form:**
  

$$
V \propto N \quad (\text{at constant } P, T)
$$

  

$$
V \propto n \quad (\text{where } n \text{ is the number of moles})
$$

* **Standard Molar Volume:** At standard temperature and pressure ($\text{STP}: T_0 = 273.15\text{ K} = 0^\circ\text{C},\; P_0 = 1.013 \times 10^5\text{ Pa} = 1\text{ atm}$):
  

$$
V_{\text{molar}} = 22.414 \times 10^{-3}\text{ m}^3\text{ mol}^{-1} = 22.414\text{ L}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

  At IUPAC standard conditions ($\text{SATP}: T = 273.15\text{ K},\; P = 1\text{ bar} = 10^5\text{ Pa}$):
  

$$
V_{\text{molar}} \approx 22.71\text{ L}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

### Section 3: Mathematical Derivations & Analytical Frameworks

***

#### Derivation 3.1: Synthesis of the Ideal Gas Equation of State
**Priority Tag:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Physical Objective:** Derive the unified relation connecting state variables $P, V, T$ and gas quantity $n$.

**Assumptions:**
1. The gas system undergoes quasi-static thermodynamic transitions across equilibrium states.
2. The gas strictly complies with the empirical relationships of Boyle, Charles, and Avogadro.

**Step-by-Step Derivation:**

Consider a fixed quantity of gas shifting from an initial equilibrium state $(P_1, V_1, T_1)$ to a final equilibrium state $(P_2, V_2, T_2)$ via a two-step virtual path:

* **Step 1 (Isothermal expansion/compression at constant $T_1$):**
  Transition from $(P_1, V_1, T_1)$ to intermediate state $(P_2, V_i, T_1)$.
  Applying Boyle's Law:
  

$$
P_1 V_1 = P_2 V_i \implies V_i = \frac{P_1 V_1}{P_2} \quad \text{--- (Equation 1)}
$$

* **Step 2 (Isobaric expansion/compression at constant $P_2$):**
  Transition from intermediate state $(P_2, V_i, T_1)$ to final state $(P_2, V_2, T_2)$.
  Applying Charles's Law:
  

$$
\frac{V_i}{T_1} = \frac{V_2}{T_2} \implies V_i = \frac{V_2 T_1}{T_2} \quad \text{--- (Equation 2)}
$$

* **Step 3 (Equating Intermediate Volume $V_i$):**
  Equating (Equation 1) and (Equation 2):
  

$$
\frac{P_1 V_1}{P_2} = \frac{V_2 T_1}{T_2}
$$

  Rearranging state variables across boundaries:
  

$$
\frac{P_1 V_1}{T_1} = \frac{P_2 V_2}{T_2} = \text{Constant}
$$

  
  Therefore, for any fixed mass of gas:
  

$$
\frac{PV}{T} = K_{\text{system}}
$$

* **Step 4 (Introducing Molar Quantity & Avogadro's Hypothesis):**
  By Avogadro's Law, at constant $P$ and $T$, $V \propto n$ (where $n$ is the number of moles). Thus, the constant $K_{\text{system}}$ is directly proportional to the number of moles:
  

$$
K_{\text{system}} = n R
$$

  where $R$ is the Universal Gas Constant.

**Final Boxed Result:**

$$
\boxed{PV = nRT}
$$

* Units: $P$ in $\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^{-2}$ ($\text{Pa}$), $V$ in $\text{m}^3$, $n$ in $\text{mol}$, $T$ in $\text{K}$, $R = 8.314\text{ J}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}\cdot\text{K}^{-1}$.

***

#### Derivation 3.2: Microscopic & Mass-Density Formulations

##### 1. Microscopic Formulation (Boltzmann Form)
Let $N$ be the total number of gas molecules and $N_A$ be Avogadro’s Number ($N_A = 6.022 \times 10^{23}\text{ molecules}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$).
The number of moles is:

$$
n = \frac{N}{N_A}
$$

Substitute into the Ideal Gas Equation:

$$
PV = \left(\frac{N}{N_A}\right) RT = N \left(\frac{R}{N_A}\right) T
$$

Define **Boltzmann's Constant ($k_B$):**

$$
k_B \equiv \frac{R}{N_A} = \frac{8.31446\text{ J}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}\cdot\text{K}^{-1}}{6.02214 \times 10^{23}\text{ mol}^{-1}} \approx 1.3806 \times 10^{-23}\text{ J}\cdot\text{K}^{-1}
$$

Thus:

$$
\boxed{PV = N k_B T}
$$

Defining the molecular number density $n_0 = \frac{N}{V}$ (number of molecules per unit volume):

$$
\boxed{P = n_0 k_B T}
$$

##### 2. Mass-Density Formulation
Let $M$ be the molar mass of the gas ($\text{kg}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$ or $\text{g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$) and $m_{\text{total}}$ be the total mass of the gas sample.

$$
n = \frac{m_{\text{total}}}{M}
$$

Substitute into $PV = nRT$:

$$
PV = \frac{m_{\text{total}}}{M} RT \implies P = \left(\frac{m_{\text{total}}}{V}\right) \frac{RT}{M}
$$

Recognizing macroscopic mass density $\rho = \frac{m_{\text{total}}}{V}$:

$$
\boxed{P = \frac{\rho R T}{M} \quad \iff \quad \rho = \frac{PM}{RT}}
$$

* **Key Deduction:** For a given ideal gas ($M = \text{constant}$), the density ratio across states satisfies:
  

$$
\frac{\rho_1}{\rho_2} = \left(\frac{P_1}{P_2}\right) \left(\frac{T_2}{T_1}\right)
$$

#### Derivation 3.3: Dalton's Law of Partial Pressures
**Priority Tag:** ⭐⭐⭐⭐

**Physical Principle:** Consider a rigid, inert gas mixture of volume $V$ containing $k$ non-reacting ideal gases at absolute temperature $T$. Let the mixture contain $n_1$ moles of gas 1, $n_2$ moles of gas 2, ..., $n_k$ moles of gas $k$.

Since each gas behaves as an ideal gas and the molecules do not exert forces on one another:
* The partial pressure $P_i$ exerted by the $i$-th component is the pressure it would exert if it alone occupied the entire volume $V$ at temperature $T$:
  

$$
P_i = \frac{n_i R T}{V}
$$

* The total number of moles in the container is:
  

$$
n_{\text{total}} = \sum_{i=1}^{k} n_i = n_1 + n_2 + \dots + n_k
$$

* The total pressure $P_{\text{total}}$ exerted by the mixture is:
  

$$
P_{\text{total}} = \frac{n_{\text{total}} R T}{V} = \frac{\left(\sum_{i=1}^k n_i\right) R T}{V} = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \frac{n_i R T}{V}
$$

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{P_{\text{total}} = P_1 + P_2 + \dots + P_k = \sum_{i=1}^{k} P_i}
$$

* **Relation to Mole Fraction ($x_i$):**
  Dividing $P_i$ by $P_{\text{total}}$:
  

$$
\frac{P_i}{P_{\text{total}}} = \frac{\frac{n_i R T}{V}}{\frac{n_{\text{total}} R T}{V}} = \frac{n_i}{n_{\text{total}}} \equiv x_i
$$

  

$$
\boxed{P_i = x_i P_{\text{total}}}
$$

  where $x_i$ is the mole fraction of component $i$, satisfying $\sum x_i = 1$.

#### Problem 1: Two Connected Rigid Bulbs (Thermal Redistribution)
**Difficulty Level:** High (CBSE Board Exam / JEE Main Archetype)

**Statement:**
Two insulated, rigid bulbs $A$ and $B$ of equal volume $V_0 = 10.0\text{ L}$ are connected by a thin tube of negligible volume containing a stopcock. Initially, the stopcock is open, and both bulbs contain an ideal gas at temperature $T_0 = 300\text{ K}$ and uniform pressure $P_0 = 1.0\text{ atm}$. The stopcock remains open while bulb $A$ is placed in an oil bath maintained at $T_A = 400\text{ K}$, and bulb $B$ is immersed in a cooling bath at $T_B = 200\text{ K}$. Calculate the final equilibrium pressure $P_f$ of the system and the number of moles in each bulb.

**Solution:**

* **Step 1: Calculate initial moles in the system.**
  Total initial volume $V_{\text{initial}} = V_A + V_B = 2V_0$.
  Total moles $n_{\text{total}}$:
  

$$
n_{\text{total}} = \frac{P_0 (2V_0)}{R T_0}
$$

* **Step 2: Formulate the final steady state condition.**
  Because the connecting tube is open, the final pressure must equalize throughout the entire apparatus:
  

$$
P_A = P_B = P_f
$$

  The final number of moles in bulbs $A$ and $B$ are:
  

$$
n_A = \frac{P_f V_0}{R T_A}, \qquad n_B = \frac{P_f V_0}{R T_B}
$$

* **Step 3: Apply conservation of mass (conservation of total moles).**
  

$$
n_A + n_B = n_{\text{total}}
$$

  

$$
\frac{P_f V_0}{R T_A} + \frac{P_f V_0}{R T_B} = \frac{P_0 (2V_0)}{R T_0}
$$

  Cancel common factors $\frac{V_0}{R}$:
  

$$
P_f \left(\frac{1}{T_A} + \frac{1}{T_B}\right) = \frac{2 P_0}{T_0}
$$

  

$$
P_f \left(\frac{T_A + T_B}{T_A T_B}\right) = \frac{2 P_0}{T_0} \implies P_f = 2 P_0 \left(\frac{T_A T_B}{T_0 (T_A + T_B)}\right)
$$

* **Step 4: Substitute the given values.**
  

$$
P_f = 2(1.0\text{ atm}) \left[\frac{(400\text{ K})(200\text{ K})}{(300\text{ K})(400\text{ K} + 200\text{ K})}\right]
$$

  

$$
P_f = 2.0 \times \frac{80000}{300 \times 600} = 2.0 \times \frac{80000}{180000} = 2.0 \times \frac{4}{9} = \frac{8}{9}\text{ atm} \approx \mathbf{0.889\text{ atm}}
$$

* **Step 5: Compute final moles in each bulb.**
  Using $V_0 = 10.0\text{ L} = 10^{-2}\text{ m}^3$, $P_f = \frac{8}{9}\text{ atm} = \frac{8}{9} \times 1.013 \times 10^5\text{ Pa} \approx 9.004 \times 10^4\text{ Pa}$:
  

$$
n_A = \frac{(9.004 \times 10^4\text{ Pa})(10^{-2}\text{ m}^3)}{(8.314\text{ J}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}\cdot\text{K}^{-1})(400\text{ K})} = \frac{900.4}{3325.6} \approx \mathbf{0.271\text{ mol}}
$$

  

$$
n_B = \frac{(9.004 \times 10^4\text{ Pa})(10^{-2}\text{ m}^3)}{(8.314\text{ J}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}\cdot\text{K}^{-1})(200\text{ K})} = \frac{900.4}{1662.8} \approx \mathbf{0.542\text{ mol}}
$$

  Notice that $n_B = 2 n_A$ since $T_A = 2 T_B$. Gas migrates from the hot bulb to the cold bulb!

#### Problem 2: Rising Underwater Air Bubble (Charles + Boyle Combination)
**Difficulty Level:** Moderate (Standard NCERT Exemplar Variant)

**Statement:**
An air bubble of volume $V_1 = 1.0\text{ cm}^3$ rises from the bottom of a deep freshwater lake of depth $h = 40.0\text{ m}$ to the surface. The temperature at the lake bed is $t_1 = 7.0^\circ\text{C}$, while the surface water temperature is $t_2 = 27.0^\circ\text{C}$. Atmospheric pressure at the surface is $P_{\text{atm}} = 1.01 \times 10^5\text{ Pa}$, the density of water is $\rho_w = 1.00 \times 10^3\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m}^{-3}$, and $g = 9.8\text{ m}\cdot\text{s}^{-2}$. Determine the volume of the bubble when it reaches the surface.

**Solution:**

* **Step 1: Determine thermodynamic conditions at the bottom (State 1).**
  

$$
T_1 = 7.0 + 273.15 = 280.15\text{ K}
$$

  Hydrostatic pressure at depth $h$:
  

$$
P_1 = P_{\text{atm}} + \rho_w g h
$$

  

$$
P_1 = 1.01 \times 10^5 + (1.00 \times 10^3)(9.8)(40.0) = 1.01 \times 10^5 + 3.92 \times 10^5 = 4.93 \times 10^5\text{ Pa}
$$

  

$$
V_1 = 1.0\text{ cm}^3 = 1.0 \times 10^{-6}\text{ m}^3
$$

* **Step 2: Determine thermodynamic conditions at the surface (State 2).**
  

$$
T_2 = 27.0 + 273.15 = 300.15\text{ K}
$$

  

$$
P_2 = P_{\text{atm}} = 1.01 \times 10^5\text{ Pa}
$$

* **Step 3: Apply the combined ideal gas law for a fixed mass of gas.**
  Since the bubble does not exchange air with the water, $n = \text{constant}$:
  

$$
\frac{P_1 V_1}{T_1} = \frac{P_2 V_2}{T_2}
$$

  Solve for $V_2$:
  

$$
V_2 = V_1 \times \left(\frac{P_1}{P_2}\right) \times \left(\frac{T_2}{T_1}\right)
$$

* **Step 4: Execute computation.**
  

$$
V_2 = (1.0\text{ cm}^3) \times \left(\frac{4.93 \times 10^5\text{ Pa}}{1.01 \times 10^5\text{ Pa}}\right) \times \left(\frac{300.15\text{ K}}{280.15\text{ K}}\right)
$$

  

$$
V_2 = 1.0 \times (4.881) \times (1.0714) \approx \mathbf{5.23\text{ cm}^3}
$$

#### Problem 3: Leaking Vessel / Faulty Seal (Variable Mass System)
**Difficulty Level:** High (NCERT Textbook / Competitive Pattern)

**Statement:**
An open vessel contains air at $t_1 = 27^\circ\text{C}$. To what temperature must the vessel be heated so that:
1. One-third of the initial mass of air escapes out?
2. One-third of the air remaining inside escapes out?
*(Assume the expansion of the vessel material is completely negligible).*

**Solution:**

Let the internal volume of the rigid vessel be $V$. Because the vessel is open to the atmosphere, the pressure inside remains equal to atmospheric pressure throughout heating:

$$
P = \text{constant} \quad \text{and} \quad V = \text{constant} \implies PV = \text{constant}
$$

From $PV = nRT$:

$$
n_1 R T_1 = n_2 R T_2 \implies n_1 T_1 = n_2 T_2
$$

where $T_1 = 27 + 273 = 300\text{ K}$.

* **Part (1): One-third of the initial air escapes.**
  The number of moles escaping is $\Delta n = \frac{1}{3} n_1$.
  Therefore, the moles remaining in the container at temperature $T_2$ are:
  

$$
n_2 = n_1 - \frac{1}{3} n_1 = \frac{2}{3} n_1
$$

  Applying the constant $nT$ relation:
  

$$
n_1 T_1 = \left(\frac{2}{3} n_1\right) T_2 \implies T_2 = \frac{3}{2} T_1
$$

  

$$
T_2 = \frac{3}{2}(300\text{ K}) = 450\text{ K}
$$

  In Celsius:
  

$$
t_2 = 450 - 273 = \mathbf{177^\circ\text{C}}
$$

* **Part (2): One-third of the air that was initially inside escapes.**
  *(Note on phrasing in NCERT Exemplar)*: "One-third of the air escapes" means the air expelled is $\frac{1}{3}$ of the original content, which is identical to Part 1 ($T_2 = 450\text{ K}$).
  However, if the question asks: *"One-third of the air at that final temperature escapes"* (i.e., the escaped volume measured at the final temperature is $\frac{1}{3}V$):
  

$$
V_{\text{expelled at } T_2} = \frac{1}{3}V \implies V_{\text{total at } T_2} = V + \frac{1}{3}V = \frac{4}{3}V
$$

  By Charles's Law for the entire original mass of gas:
  

$$
\frac{V}{T_1} = \frac{\frac{4}{3}V}{T_2} \implies T_2 = \frac{4}{3} T_1 = \frac{4}{3}(300) = \mathbf{400\text{ K}} = \mathbf{127^\circ\text{C}}
$$

### Section 5: Examiner Traps & Common Student Mistakes

***

| No. | Pitfall Category | The Common Student Mistake | Physical Reality & Corrective Method |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 1 | **Temperature Scale Error** | Substituting temperature in Celsius into $PV=nRT$ or Charles's Law (e.g., $T_2/T_1 = 40^\circ\text{C}/20^\circ\text{C} = 2$). | **Fatal Error.** All thermodynamic gas laws depend on kinetic theory where $T$ is the absolute mean kinetic measure. **Always convert to Kelvin:** $T(\text{K}) = t(^\circ\text{C}) + 273.15$. A change $\Delta T$ is the same in K and $^\circ\text{C}$, but ratios require absolute Kelvin. |
| 2 | **Universal Gas Constant ($R$) Inconsistency** | Using $R = 8.314$ while pressure is in atmospheres and volume is in liters. | **Dimensional Inconsistency.** 
• If using SI: $P$ in $\text{Pa}$, $V$ in $\text{m}^3 \implies R = 8.314\text{ J}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}\cdot\text{K}^{-1}$.
• If using practical units: $P$ in $\text{atm}$, $V$ in $\text{L} \implies R \approx 0.0821\text{ L}\cdot\text{atm}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}\cdot\text{K}^{-1}$. |
| 3 | **Open Vessel Pressure Assumption** | Assuming the pressure changes when an open container is heated. | In an open vessel, gas expands freely into the atmosphere. The pressure is **pinned to ambient atmospheric pressure** ($P = \text{constant}$). Heating causes the mass of gas in the vessel to decrease ($n T = \text{constant}$). |
| 4 | **Gauge Pressure vs. Absolute Pressure** | Using tyre gauge pressure directly as $P$ in $PV = nRT$. | Tyres/Bourdon gauges measure pressure relative to atmospheric pressure: $P_{\text{gauge}} = P_{\text{abs}} - P_{\text{atm}}$. **Always use absolute pressure:** $P_{\text{abs}} = P_{\text{gauge}} + P_{\text{atm}}$. |
| 5 | **Molar Mass Units in Density Equation** | Substituting $M$ in $\text{g}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$ alongside SI density $\rho$ in $\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^{-3}$. | In $\rho = \frac{PM}{RT}$, if $P$ is in $\text{Pa}$ and $R = 8.314$, $M$ **must be in $\text{kg}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$**. For $\text{O}_2$: $M = 32 \times 10^{-3}\text{ kg}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}$, not $32$. |

### Section 6: Speed Hacks, Golden Points & Summary Formulas

#### 1. Instantaneous Microscopic Conversions
* Number of moles from total molecules:
  

$$
n = \frac{N}{N_A} = \frac{m_{\text{total}}}{M}
$$

* Relationship between $R$, $k_B$, and $N_A$:
  

$$
R = N_A k_B \quad \implies \quad \frac{R}{k_B} = N_A \approx 6.022 \times 10^{23}
$$

#### 2. The Ratio Form of Ideal Gas Equation
When solving transition problems from State 1 to State 2, write the ratio directly to cancel out constants:

$$
\frac{P_1 V_1}{P_2 V_2} = \frac{n_1 T_1}{n_2 T_2} = \frac{m_1 T_1}{m_2 T_2}
$$

* Constant Mass, Isochoric: $\frac{P_1}{P_2} = \frac{T_1}{T_2}$
* Constant Mass, Isothermal: $\frac{P_1}{P_2} = \frac{V_2}{V_1}$
* Constant Mass, Isobaric: $\frac{V_1}{V_2} = \frac{T_1}{T_2}$

#### 3. Quick Value Conversions for Numerical Problems
* At STP ($0^\circ\text{C}, 1\text{ atm}$):
  

$$
1\text{ mol of gas} \approx 22.4\text{ L} = 22.4 \times 10^{-3}\text{ m}^3
$$

* Universal Constant Products:
  

$$
R \times (300\text{ K}) \approx 2494.2\text{ J}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1} \approx 2.5\times 10^3\text{ J}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

  

$$
\frac{R}{1.013 \times 10^5} \approx 8.205 \times 10^{-5}\text{ m}^3\cdot\text{K}^{-1}\cdot\text{mol}^{-1}
$$

* Density of Dry Air at STP:
  

$$
\rho_{\text{air, STP}} \approx 1.29\text{ kg}\cdot\text{m}^{-3}
$$

#### 4. Slopes on Thermodynamic Planes
* For Boyle's Law ($P$ vs $1/V$):
  

$$
\text{Slope} = nRT \implies \text{Steeper slope indicates higher temperature } T.
$$

* For Charles's Law ($V$ vs $T$):
  

$$
\text{Slope} = \frac{nR}{P} \implies \text{Steeper slope indicates lower pressure } P.
$$

* For Gay-Lussac's Law ($P$ vs $T$):
  

$$
\text{Slope} = \frac{nR}{V} \implies \text{Steeper slope indicates smaller volume } V.
$$
